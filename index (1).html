Recommendation

Do not commit at launch terms. Re-engage if term debt comes down to about $2.9bn (≤ 7.0x net Underwriting EBITDA), most likely through flex if order books are thin.

The franchise is strong and the price is fair, but $3.15bn of term debt is 7.8x our Underwriting EBITDA of $404mm, 2–3 turns above recent LBOs. The credit depends on one variable, gross margin, and the evidence on it is mixed. We weighted four margin outlooks, from "margins hold" to "core erosion continues", with weights fixed before running the model. The probability-weighted seven-year PD is 41% against a 25% limit, about the historical default rate of a B-rated issuer. Even the most optimistic weighting gives 30%. The spread more than covers expected loss, so the loan fails on default risk, which wider pricing cannot fix. At $2.9bn the weighted PD falls to 23%. Almost every simulated default is a failure to refinance at the 2033 maturity, not a missed payment.

02

The business and its credit merits

What it does. OPCH dispenses and administers infusion therapies in patients' homes and in its own ambulatory infusion suites, as a lower-cost alternative to a hospital stay. Its therapies include anti-infectives, nutrition support, immunoglobulin, and specialty and rare-disease drugs. FY2025 revenue was $5.65bn, up 13%. Most of it is the drug itself, passed through at cost plus a margin, so gross margin is thin (19.3%) and sensitive to therapy mix.

Scale and footprint. OPCH operates 196 locations in 43 states: 87 full-service compounding pharmacies (73 with infusion suites) and 109 stand-alone ambulatory infusion suites, staffed by more than 5,000 clinicians.

Credit merits: why a lender would want this loan
  • Market leader with a moat. It has the largest independent national network of sterile compounding pharmacies and suites. That network is slow and costly to replicate, and the rest of the market is fragmented.
  • Non-cyclical demand. Chronic and acute infusion therapy cannot be deferred in a recession, and revenue grew in every period we reviewed.
  • Payers want it. Home infusion saves payers money against inpatient care, and OPCH has contracts across managed care, Medicare and Medicaid.
  • Real equity beneath us. $2.87bn (48%) of cash equity ranks behind all debt.
  • A strategic owner. The ~49% co-investor is a large healthcare company whose drug purchasing scale could ease the gross-margin pressure that drives our risk case. It has also set out a framework to buy the sponsor's stake (Section 11). None of this is in our EBITDA, because it is not yet earned.
  • Strong cash conversion today. Pre-deal interest coverage is 7.5x, and capex is under 1% of revenue.
What can hurt it
  • Mix and drug pricing. Biosimilar adoption and higher-cost rare-disease drugs compress gross margin (Section 5).
  • Growth has stalled. Underwriting EBITDA was flat over the last twelve months ($405mm FY25 vs $404mm LTM). 2026 revenue guidance implies only 0.5–2.2% growth.
  • Concentration. The largest payer is ~14% of revenue, and ~12% is reimbursed directly by government programmes.
  • Competition. Infusion units owned by large insurers and pharmacy chains compete for the same referrals and payer contracts.

This franchise can support a leveraged loan. The question is how much debt, given margins that have fallen for two years and growth that has slowed.

We then tested every management add-back against five criteria (CARVE): did it hit cash, is it auditable, is it non-recurring, is it verifiable, and is it excluded from running the business? An add-back that fails any test is rejected. Irregular gains are stripped out as strictly as losses would be added back.

04

Leverage against the market

Measured the way the market measures it, on management's Adjusted EBITDA, leverage is 6.7x. That compares with a 2025 average of about 5.0x for syndicated LBO loans and 4.8–5.3x for 2025–26 originations. It is also above the 6.0x level that the 2013 interagency leveraged-lending guidance flagged as raising concerns for most industries. The OCC and FDIC withdrew that guidance and its 2014 FAQs on December 5, 2025, so 6.0x is no longer a supervisory line for national banks. We keep it as a market reference point because lenders and rating agencies still anchor to it.

A rating agency sees the same problem. On October 8, 2026 it placed OPCH's 'BB-' issuer rating on CreditWatch Negative and signalled a possible downgrade of several notches. It measured pre-deal leverage at 2.6x on its own adjusted basis (we measure 2.9x on Underwriting EBITDA) and expects the new structure to go well past its 4.0x downgrade trigger. A multi-notch move from BB- lands at B or B-, which is why this memo benchmarks default risk and pricing against B-rated credits.

On our Underwriting EBITDA, leverage is 7.8x. Launch interest coverage of 1.8x is below the 2.2–2.3x of 2024–26 vintages. The 48% equity cheque is in line with recent deals, not unusually thick.

05

Why margins are falling

Underwriting EBITDA margin fell 127 bps from FY2023 to LTM, from 8.36% to 7.09%. It breaks down exactly as follows: gross margin fell 375 bps, SG&A efficiency recovered 211 bps, and other items added 37 bps. Management attributes the gross margin decline to three things:

  • therapy mix shifting to higher-cost rare and orphan drugs
  • biosimilar adoption
  • patient attrition in its chronic inflammatory disease (CID) portfolio

In February management guided to a $25–35mm 2026 gross-profit headwind from CID. By the second quarter it had raised that to about $55mm.

The margin percentage overstates the damage. Most revenue is drug passed through at cost, so a shift to expensive therapies dilutes the percentage even when gross profit dollars grow. Underwriting EBITDA rose 12%, from $360mm in FY2023 to $405mm in FY2025, while revenue rose 31%. What is new is that EBITDA dollars stopped growing over the last twelve months: $404mm LTM against $405mm in FY2025. That is why the margin trend matters now.

Cash flow and stress tests

We project seven years on Underwriting EBITDA. The model includes:

  • floating-rate interest
  • cash taxes with the 30% interest-deduction limit
  • capex at 0.75% of revenue
  • working capital
  • 1% annual amortisation
  • a 50% sweep of each year's excess cash flow to the term loan

07

Monte Carlo: probability of default

Why we ran it. Three fixed cases show what can happen, not how likely it is. The committee needs a probability of default to price the loan and apply a risk limit. We ran 10,000 seven-year paths through the same debt schedule as the Excel model, after confirming the R engine reproduces Excel exactly.

What moves on each path:

  • Revenue growth: lender base ± 3% a year.
  • SOFR: starts at today's 3.90% and drifts back toward 3.50%, with 75 bps annual volatility.
  • EBITDA margin: random shocks of 40 bps a year around each scenario's trend, 85% of which carry into the next year. OPCH's three annual moves (−61, −59, −8 bps) scatter about 30 bps around their average; 40 bps allows for the short history. The trend enters only through each scenario's drift, so the observed decline is not counted twice. A 60 bps volatility case is reported as a sensitivity.
  • Growth and margin shocks are correlated (0.3).

What counts as default. Either cash runs out after the revolver is fully drawn, or net leverage is above 6.0x at the 2033 maturity, so the loan cannot be refinanced. On our stricter EBITDA, 6.0x equals about 5.2x on the market's adjusted basis, roughly the average new LBO. In other words, the test asks whether OPCH can refinance at average market leverage. A year of fixed-charge coverage below 1.0x is a cash shortfall the revolver can bridge, not a default.

How we decide, fixed before running. The evidence on margins points in different directions, so no single scenario decides. We weighted four outlooks:

  • Margins hold, 20%. The strategic owner's purchasing scale helps and the biosimilar mix settles.
  • Latest-year pace (−8 bps a year), 30%. This is the most recent observation.
  • Recent decline repeats (−127 bps by 2033), 30%. Two of the three observed years fell about 60 bps.
  • Core operating pace (−25 bps a year), 20%. This is the latest year excluding volatile items, plus the raised CID headwind.

The rule is: weighted seven-year PD ≤ 25% and spread at least 150 bps above annual expected loss. The 25% limit sits at or below the historical seven-year default rate of a B-rated issuer (28.6% over 1920–2006 and 35.5% over 1983–2006 in one major rating agency's study). The loan can be no riskier than the B credit its spread pays for.

Sensitivities: decline at twice the recent pace gives a PD of 94.4%. Flat margins with 60 bps volatility, our earlier calibration, give 17.2%.

Does the verdict depend on the weights? No. The weighted PD is 30% on an optimistic weighting and 50% on a cautious one. Both are above the limit.

Does it depend on the threshold? Refinancing at 2033 is the binding risk, so we tested the leverage at which lenders would refinance. Even at 7.0x, about 6.0x on the market's basis and above today's average, the decline-repeats PD is 31%.

Is a refinancing failure a loss? Not always. Many such loans end in an amend-and-extend at a higher spread. If lenders extend anything up to 7.0x without losing principal, the weighted probability of a principal loss is 23%. That is just inside the limit, but only on the most generous reading of the 2033 market.

Exposure at default: putting PD, EAD and LGD together

Expected loss is the product of three pieces: EL = PD × EAD × LGD. Exposure at default (EAD) is what is still owed when default happens. Our term loan amortises 1% a year and is paid down by the cash sweep, and almost every default comes at the 2033 maturity. So on a defaulted path we are owed about 88% of original par, not 100%. The revolver is assumed fully drawn at default ($500mm). Because it ranks equally with our loan, every dollar it draws is a dollar that dilutes our recovery.

Expected and unexpected loss

On every simulated path, loss is the term loan outstanding at default times one minus recovery, as a share of original par. Expected loss is the average across all paths and belongs in the price. Unexpected loss is how far losses can run beyond that average, and it must be absorbed by capital. The weighted distribution pools the four margin scenarios at their decision weights.

The loss profile is all-or-nothing. The spread pays for the expected loss of about 1.8% a year, but in one outcome in a hundred we lose more than half the loan. That concentrated tail is an argument for a smaller loan, and at the very least a smaller hold.

The strategic investor's takeout option

The strategic investor has disclosed a framework to acquire the sponsor's stake later, subject to conditions and approvals; timing and price are not public. A change of control would most likely repay our loan at par. We overlaid a takeout on the simulated paths, with three conditions:

  • It happens with a given probability between 2029 and 2031.
  • It happens only if net leverage is at or below 7.0x that year, since an acquirer buys a healthy business.
  • It rescues a path only if it comes before default.

Our options, what would change our view, and next steps

The banks have committed up to $3.15bn, so a participating fund cannot set the structure. It can only choose whether to commit, how much, and at what price. Weak demand at launch can lead to "flex", where debt is moved into equity or terms are tightened. That is the realistic route to the structure we can support.

Methodology and validation

How the work fits together
  • Excel credit model: historicals, EBITDA bridge, sources and uses, capital structure, seven-year projections, credit statistics and recovery waterfall.
  • R, independent analysis: reverse stress test, Monte Carlo PD, loss given default, loss distribution, debt sizing, margin decomposition and takeout overlay.
  • The R engines read their inputs from the Excel file, so one set of assumptions drives everything.
How we checked it
  • 126 of 126 filing inputs match the filings exactly. EBITDA and Adjusted EBITDA tie to the company in every period, sources equal uses, and EV matches the announced ~$5.8bn.
  • R is validated against Excel before every run. Each R script first rebuilds the debt schedule and stops unless it reproduces the Excel model to within 0.01 (term loan balance, minimum FCCR and DSCR, liquidity, recovery). Only then does it simulate. The weighted results are checked against the scenario results they are built from.
  • Expected loss from the loss distribution equals the Monte Carlo figure, and unexpected loss equals its analytic formula.
Changes after senior review (v2)
  • SOFR updated to the current 3.90% fixing (it was 3.50%).
  • Lender base growth lowered to match 2026 guidance (it was 4–5%).
  • Margin volatility recalibrated around the trend (40 bps; it was 60 bps) to avoid counting the decline twice.
  • The decision moved from one scenario to a probability-weighted view, with weights set before re-running.
  • The 25% limit is now tied to B-rated default history, market benchmarks are updated to Q3-26, and credit merits and realistic options have been added.
  • The corrections made the result worse, not better: the proposed structure's weighted PD is 41%. The maximum supportable debt is unchanged at $2.9bn.

Limitations: public filings only (no general ledger or quality-of-earnings report). Loan terms are modelled until syndication. Interest is computed on opening balances. The revolver's own maturity is flagged but not modelled as a default trigger.
